WHAT I WANTED TO CREATE

ScanCompass gives away a free external website-security assessment, and I am
building a paid Extended Security Assessment to go with it. On 13 October 2026
I asked for both offerings to be publicly visible on the site by 15 October, so
that I could point startup and university applications at a real page.

What I wanted was a prelaunch page, not a shop. It had to say clearly that the
free assessment works today and that the paid one does not exist yet. It had to
have no price, no buy button, no checkout and no sign-up form, because none of
those things existed and I did not want to promise them. It also had to be
honest about the payment side: our hosted checkout had been tested in the
payment provider's test mode only, and no real payment was possible.

WHAT I DID AND DECIDED

The page itself took one day. It came to exactly eight files: a new page, a new
text-only component, a new copy module, one link in the site header, one word in
the shared interface copy, one block of style rules, and two new test files. Not
one file in the scanner, the server, the database or the report path was
touched. That is the narrow, proven fact: the source of the assessment, its
scoring and its report was unchanged, and after the deployment a real free
assessment completed with both downloads working. I am not claiming that every
possible assessment is byte-for-byte identical to before.

TO BE CLEAR ABOUT WHO WROTE WHAT: the code, the tests, the release procedure and
the operator scripts were written by an AI coding assistant working under my
direction, and separate AI sessions reviewed them. I set the objective, decided
what the page would and would not say, authorised each step, ran the production
deployment myself and accepted it. I am not claiming to have typed the eight
files. The full breakdown is in section 18 of the technical note.

I decided what the page would say and what it would not. I approved naming the
payment provider publicly in one sentence, in order to state truthfully that its
checkout had only been tested in test mode. I approved showing four examples of
what the paid assessment is planned to look at, labelled as planned rather than
delivered. I approved explaining that the price would be shown before a customer
confirmed a payment, without publishing a price. I approved a Plans link in the
header at every screen width. I approved presentation only: no purchase control,
no payment flag, no live provider configuration and no deployment.

Then I made the decision that cost me the deadline. The page lived on a branch
of the newer development work, and that work needs a database table production
does not have. A build of that branch, started against a production-shaped
database, reported its data layer as unavailable WHILE ITS HEALTH CHECK STILL
ANSWERED OK. That is the worst kind of failure, because nothing looks broken. I
decided not to deploy the branch, and to build the release instead from the
exact source production was already running, with only the two interface
commits added on top. That release differs from what was running in eight
files and nothing else.

I also decided how the release itself would be controlled: a written procedure
of numbered blocks, each of which must fail closed; three separate
authorisations from me rather than one, each tied to exact values so that an
approval could not be reused for something else; and a rollback prepared and
pre-authorised in advance, so that nobody would have to ask permission in the
middle of a failure.

PROBLEMS I RAN INTO

1. THE PAGE WAS NOT THE PROBLEM. Six rounds of independent review looked at
this work, and not one of them found a defect in the eight application files.
Every finding was in the release procedure. One review did find a copy mistake:
the page said none of the buying steps could be done today, which was too
strong, because existing account holders can already sign in and verify a
domain. I had that one sentence narrowed and its tests re-pinned.

2. THE PROCEDURE WAS WRONG SIX TIMES. In order, the reviews found: a rollback
that trusted a tag which can be moved; a file transfer whose integrity was
never proved; instructions that printed the word "stop" and then carried on,
because printing a message is not stopping; a build whose failure was hidden
because its exit status came from the command it was piped into; helpers that
could not stop their own block; a rollback that left the approvals still usable,
so a rolled-back release could be accepted anyway; a failed check that only
PRINTED that a rollback was needed, so clearing the fault and re-running could
be accepted without ever rolling back; a variable inherited from my own shell
that turned every mismatch into "ok"; and a rollback state that could not be
recorded at all if the disk was full or the directory had become unwritable.

3. THE FIRST REAL PRODUCTION ATTEMPT STOPPED, AND THE CHECK WAS WRONG, NOT THE
DATABASE. On 15 October everything passed until a check that reads the payment
era. The database correctly reported era 1. The check demanded a line reading
exactly "ERA 1", and the database's default output format pads the value, so
the line actually read " ERA 1" with one leading space. A correct database was
rejected over one space. The reason the tests had never caught it is the part
worth remembering: the test harness used a fake database client, and the fake
printed the line without padding. The check and the fake agreed with each
other, not with the real program.

4. FIXING IT COST THE WHOLE RUN. The fix changed a shared library, the library's
hash changed, and my authorisation was bound to that hash. Nothing carried
over: not the build, not the server preparation, not the approval. Everything
had to be done again from the beginning.

5. THE CAREFUL INSTINCT WOULD HAVE WRECKED THE RELEASE. After the image was
loaded on 16 October, the obvious thing to do before switching was to re-run the
baseline checks. A review pointed out that starting any block deliberately
invalidates the later blocks' records, so re-running the baseline would have
destroyed the frozen record of the load, and the load block refuses to run twice
while the image is already there. The safe-looking action was the dangerous one.

6. ONE TEST FAILED ON THE BUILD. The first run of the test gate reported 8,623
passed, 1 failed, 610 skipped. The failing test expected no log output and saw
42 lines, which fits interference from other tests running at the same time;
that file passed all 55 of its own tests on its own. One unchanged retry passed
with 8,624 passed, 0 failed. I am recording both results, because the release
did not have zero failures across all attempts.

HOW THEY WERE RESOLVED

Each review round was answered by changing only the procedure and its tests,
never the eight application files, and the release tree was re-verified
unchanged every time.

The era check was fixed test-first. The new cases were run against the old
procedure and thirteen of them failed, which is what writing the test first is
for. Two of those failures mattered: it reproduced the exact production stop,
and it exposed a SECOND, OPPOSITE defect nobody had been looking for. Had the
output not been padded, the old check would also have accepted a report
containing era 1 together with era 2, or era 1 twice. The check was
simultaneously too fussy about spaces and far too relaxed about what else the
report could say. The corrected check accepts the report only if it holds
exactly one era line and that line is era 1, spaces and tabs allowed. A
separate reviewer reproduced the padded ERA-1 output against a real database.
The ERA-1 and SQL-error fixtures matched; the ERA-2 fixture was later found
to be synthetic, not a genuine database capture.

The rollback was made terminal. A failure after the switch now records that
state permanently BEFORE it reports anything, and while that state exists every
block except the rollback refuses to run, including the recording of manual
results. The reproducer that used to end in a false acceptance was tested and
now ends in refusals.

For the frozen-record trap, the answer was a separate read-only preflight that
proves the same facts without starting any block of the procedure.

On 16 October the image was transferred, its identity proved against the build
host's and loaded under its own tag, with no existing tag moved. Then the
procedure stopped, because that is what my authorisation said. The site did not
change: /plans still answered 404 for three more days, with the new application
sitting on the server started by nothing.

On 19 October I ran the live preflight myself, from a deliberately empty
environment so that nothing inherited from my own shell could forge a success
message. It passed with exit status 0 and 104 minutes still left in the window:
the server state was exactly as frozen on 16 October, the running application
was still the old one, the environment hashes and payment safeguards were
unchanged, all fourteen payment routes answered 404, /plans still answered 404,
and a fresh read-only database check passed with 18 checks, reported era 1, and
produced a reconciliation report byte-identical to the baseline.

I then gave the deployment authorisation, bound to five exact values, after
being told the things that were not perfect: the one flaky test and its retry,
a runtime version one patch ahead of production's, two staging and
window deviations by an assistant session, and the fact that the baseline being
compared against was three days old rather than newly made.

The switch replaced THE APPLICATION CONTAINER ALONE. The automated acceptance
passed: /plans answered 200 with the right copy and no purchase control, the
data layer was ready on the old schema, payments were still disabled, the
database audit still passed at era 1, exactly one tag had moved, and the
database, scanner and maintenance images were untouched.

Then I did the seven checks that only a person in a browser can do: the Plans
page shows Coming soon with no purchase control; an anonymous free assessment
completes; both report downloads work; signing in with an existing account
works; domain management works with no purchase button; there are no browser
console or security-policy errors; and the phone layout and Plans navigation
work. All seven passed, and I recorded each one. The procedure allows those
results only after automated acceptance and discards old entries if that block
is re-run. These safeguards preserve the order of the evidence, but cannot
observe what I did in the browser.

The acceptance record was frozen, I gave a second, separate authorisation bound
to that frozen record, and the final block passed: the running application is
the authorised one, its tag points at it, and the rollback image is still
there. The state check printed ACCEPTED: J, JM and K passed.

THE RESULT

The page is live. The free assessment works, and its relevant code was
unchanged. The Extended Security Assessment is presented as Coming soon, with
no price, no purchase and
no checkout. Payments are disabled, all fourteen payment routes answer 404, the
database schema is untouched, and migration 0005 was not applied. The previous
application image is retained as the rollback target, and the rollback was never
needed.

The pull request is still a draft and still unmerged. What went to production
was the old source plus eight files, not that branch, and a deployment is not a
merge.

The page went live on 19 October. I had asked for 15 October. I am recording the
miss rather than the intention: the delay was the price of not deploying an
application that expected a table production did not have.
